Thin lens

Where a lens forms an image: the object distance, the image distance and the focal length, any two of which give the third.

Object to image: 49 cm. A positive image distance is a real image, on the far side of the lens, which a screen can catch; a negative one is virtual, on the object’s side, and only seen through the lens.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the distance from the object to the lens.
  2. Set the right ring at the distance from the lens to the sharp image on the screen.
  3. Read the focal length where the line crosses the middle scale.
  4. To see one lens at work, set the line through its focal length, tick “Hold the lens” and move the object.

Remarks

Find infinity first. On every scale here it is an ordinary tick, a third of the way up the drawing, a place you can point at. The object scale stops there, since a real object stands in front of the lens; the image and focal-length scales run on below it. A calculator has no such place. Ask it for the image of an object 10 cm from a 10 cm lens and it reports an error; move the object a millimetre either way and it jumps from about ten metres on one side of the lens to about ten metres on the other. The chart puts the same event in one smooth stroke. Hold the lens at 10 cm and bring the object in from far away: the image ring sinks, crosses ∞ and keeps going, into the negative distances of the virtual image you see in a magnifying glass. Nothing about the light breaks at the focal point. Only the arithmetic of distances does.

The ruler under each scale is not distance but its reciprocal, which opticians call vergence and measure in dioptres. That is why the ticks crowd. Everything from 2 metres to infinity fits in the bottom half-dioptre of the object scale, a fortieth of its length, while the single centimetre from 5 to 6 cm takes nearly seven times as much. For a lens, far is nearly all the same distance, and near is where the work is. A camera lens focuses by moving away from the sensor, by f²/(u − f) for an object at u, which for distant objects is nearly proportional to 1/u. So the distance marks on a focusing ring crowd toward ∞ for the same reason these ticks do.

Then hold the lens and watch the line. Every position it can take passes through one point on the middle scale, so a single lens is a single point, and focusing it is turning the line about that point. When the line is level the object and image distances are equal, each twice the focal length, and the image is the same size as the object. The readout shows the object-to-image distance: as you turn the line away from level in either direction it grows. Level is the shortest it can be, four focal lengths. Put the candle and the screen any closer and no position of this lens will focus one on the other.

The usual nomogram for this formula is a different drawing: three evenly graduated scales from one corner, one across, one up and the answer on the diagonal. That one is good for computing and hides the reciprocal in its geometry. This one prints the reciprocal in its ticks, where you can see it.

Formula

1/u + 1/v = 1/f, the Gaussian thin-lens equation, with u the object distance, v the image distance, negative for a virtual image, and f the focal length, negative for a diverging lens. Each term is a vergence: a reciprocal distance, in dioptres when the distance is in metres. With distances in centimetres the equation reads 100/u + 100/v = 100/f, and those three terms are what the rulers carry. Magnification is −v/u.

Construction

Every sheet on this site is drawn the same way, and this one shows the steps plainly.

  1. Write the formula as a sum of terms, each holding one variable: here 100/u + 100/v = 100/f.
  2. Give each outer variable a vertical scale on which height is proportional to its term. Here both outer scales are the same ruler, −10 to 20 dioptres, so they get the same height for the same term and infinity, 0 dioptres, lands at the same height on both.
  3. Place the third scale where a straight line averages the two outer heights in the right proportion. Equal outer rulers put it exactly halfway, and since halfway is the average of two heights, its ruler is half as long per dioptre: it reads the sum.
  4. Label every tick with the quantity a reader knows, here centimetres, not with the term it stands for.

Sheet 01 uses unequal rulers, so its middle scale is not halfway; Sheet 02 has two middle scales for two formulas. The construction is otherwise the same.
