Bicycle gears

The gear ratio of a chainring and a rear sprocket, and how far one turn of the pedals carries the bicycle. Two scales stand parallel; the third runs corner to corner, from zero to infinity, and every pair of rings that makes the same gear crosses it at one point.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the number of teeth on the chainring, the toothed ring the pedals turn.
  2. Set the right ring at the number of teeth on the rear sprocket. This scale counts down the page: few teeth at the top.
  3. Read the gear ratio where the line crosses the diagonal, on its upper side, and the distance the bicycle travels for one turn of the pedals on its lower side.
  4. Watch the faint lines. Each is another chainring and sprocket in range that make exactly the same gear; they all pass through the same point on the diagonal.

Remarks

The sheets before this one stand three scales side by side, and a ratio like this one would have gone on them as a sum of logarithms. Nomograms need not be drawn that way. This one keeps the teeth on plain rulers, one equal space for every tooth, and stands the answer on a diagonal between them. Draftsmen call the shape a Z chart, or an N chart, after the letter its three scales make. It works because the two tooth scales are parallel: the index line cuts two similar triangles off them, one at each corner, and the diagonal is divided between them in the ratio of their sides, which is the ratio of the teeth.

That proof fixes where zero must stand. Each tooth ruler has to start at the corner where it meets the diagonal: the chainring scale at the bottom left, the sprocket scale at the top right. Look at the faint stretches below 22 teeth on the left and above 10 on the right. They are paper a parallel chart would have trimmed off, and here it cannot, because sliding a scale away from its corner would break the triangles. The diagonal pays for it differently. With nothing at one end and no sprocket at the other, it runs from a ratio of 0 to a ratio of infinity, both printed at the corners, and every gear on this chart fits in half its length.

Now set 48 and 16. The line crosses the diagonal at 3, and eight faint lines cross it there too: 30 and 10, 39 and 13, 54 and 18. A calculator would make nine divisions and print 3.00 nine times; the chart shows one point with nine lines through it. A gear is a point, not a pair of numbers, and a ratio in lowest terms is the slope of a line turning about it. Set 50 and 17 and there are no faint lines, because 50 and 17 share no factor and 100 teeth is off the scale. Across the whole chart, 945 pairs of teeth make only 726 different gears. The same thing happens on a real bicycle with two or three chainrings: a triple with eight sprockets advertises 24 gears and usually has 16 to 18 distinct ones.

Hold the chainring at 50 and step the sprocket one tooth at a time. Every tooth is the same length on the right-hand scale, but the crossing jumps far at the top of it and creeps at the bottom: from 11 teeth to 12 changes the gear by 9 percent, from 27 to 28 by under 4. That is why cassettes are built with one-tooth gaps between their small sprockets and wider gaps between their large ones. Here the chart is honest only halfway, and it is worth seeing where. The diagonal is not a logarithmic scale, so its lengths are not percentages. From 13 teeth to 15 is a 15.4 percent change; from 20 to 23 is 15.0 percent, slightly less, and yet it draws the longer jump. The Z chart prints the ratio itself, plainly; to see the steps as a rider feels them, as shares, the ratio has to go back onto logarithmic parallel scales. Each form shows one thing and hides another.

Formula

Gear ratio k = a / c, for a chainring teeth and c sprocket teeth. Distance per pedal turn, or metres of development, is D = k × π d; for a road wheel about 670 mm across with its tyre, one wheel turn is 2.105 m. Gear inches are k × d in inches, here 26.4; speed in km/h at 90 pedal turns a minute is D × 5.4.

Construction: with the chainring scale at height m₁a above the bottom-left corner and the sprocket scale at depth m₂c below the top-right corner, the line through them crosses the diagonal at the fraction t = k / (k + m₂/m₁) of its length from the bottom-left corner. Here m₂/m₁ = 56/36, the ratio of the two rulers’ full lengths in teeth, so 1:1 sits at 0.39 of the way and 3:1 at 0.66. The ratio and distance graduations are placed by that fraction.
