RC time constant

How fast a capacitor charges through a resistor, from the two parts. The rulers carry only the values a parts drawer holds, twelve to a decade, and on a logarithmic ruler those values fall almost evenly. Almost: five of them stand off the line, and the chart shows where.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the resistor, in ohms (k for thousands, M for millions).
  2. Set the right ring at the capacitor, in farads (n for billionths, µ for millionths).
  3. Read the time constant where the line crosses the middle scale, on its right. On its left, read the cutoff frequency of the same two parts used as a low-pass or high-pass filter.
  4. To find parts for a time constant you need, put the line through it and look for other ring positions whose line passes through the same point; the readout lists the pairs that come within 5 percent with the resistor in the same decade.

Remarks

Look at the outer rulers before you move anything. They are logarithmic, so the paper from 1k to 10k is the same as from 10k to 100k, and the twelve values in each decade stand at nearly equal steps. That is not the chart’s doing. The standard values, the E12 series of IEC 60063, were chosen as a geometric progression: each is about 21 percent above the last, the twelfth root of ten, so on a ruler that measures ratios they are already evenly spaced. A designer who reaches into the drawer is picking points off a logarithmic scale whether they know it or not, and the drawer is the ruler printed in parts.

Now look at the short ticks on the inner side of each ruler. They are where exact twelfths of a decade fall. Most of the values sold sit on them: 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 5.6 and 6.8 are the twelfth roots of ten rounded to two figures. Five are not. 2.7, 3.3, 3.9 and 4.7 stand above their marks, where the arithmetic gives 2.6, 3.2, 3.8 and 4.6, and 8.2 stands below the 8.3 it should be. So the steps are not equal: 2.2 to 2.7 is a step of 23 percent and 3.3 to 3.9 one of 18. These are values that were in use before the series was standardized, and the committee kept them.

The chart shows what that costs. Put the line through 10k and 100 nF; it crosses 1 ms. On an exact series, any two rings moved the same number of steps in opposite directions would keep the line through that point, because a step up on one ruler and a step down on the other cancel. Move them a step at a time: 12k with 82 nF gives 0.98 ms, 15k with 68 nF 1.02, 18k with 56 nF 1.01, 22k with 47 nF 1.03, 27k with 39 nF 1.05, and 33k with 33 nF 1.09. The line walks up the middle scale as the rings pass through the region where the old values stand high. At 33 and 33 both rings sit on high values at once, and the line misses by 9 percent.

Then tick the tolerance box. Each ring gets a bar for its ±10 percent, and on a logarithmic ruler a fixed percentage is a fixed length, so the bar is the same size at 1k as at 1M. The red band between the two bars is every line the real parts could make, and where it crosses the middle scale is the range of the answer: 0.81 to 1.21 times the nominal time constant. That band is about as long as one bar, though it stands for twice the spread, because the middle scale is drawn at half size.

The bars also show why twelve values are enough. Ten percent either side of each value reaches about to the next value’s bar, so the series covers the decade with bars that just meet: 1.8 at its top and 2.2 at its bottom both reach 1.98. The fit is not perfect. The exact spacing would be met by bars of 9.56 percent, and because the historic values break the geometry, there are two small gaps where no E12 part at ±10 percent can land, from 1.32 to 1.35 and from 2.42 to 2.43. 2.7 and 3.3 just meet, at 2.97, as 1.8 and 2.2 do; elsewhere neighbouring bars overlap.

So the band answers a practical question. A 12k is one step from a 10k, about 20 percent, and the band of either one reaches nearly to the other. Choosing between neighbouring values to hit a time constant is pointless with 10 percent parts unless you measure them first.

Formula

τ = RC, with τ in seconds, R in ohms and C in farads. The capacitor’s voltage, charging from zero toward a supply V, is V(1 − e−t/τ): 63.2 percent of the way at t = τ, 95.0 at 3τ and 99.3 at 5τ. The cutoff frequency of the same parts used as a first-order filter, where the output has fallen to 1/√2 of the input, is f = 1/(2πτ), so 1 ms is 159 Hz. The three rulers are log R, log C and log τ; the outer two carry equal weight, so the time-constant scale stands halfway and reads their sum at half the size. Its six decades, 1 µs to 1 s, fill the paper that each outer ruler’s three decades fill. The E12 values are 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.7, 3.3, 3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8 and 8.2 in every decade; the inner ticks are at 10n/12.
