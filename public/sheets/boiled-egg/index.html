Boiling an egg

How long an egg must sit in boiling water for the edge of its yolk to reach a chosen temperature. The middle scale is graduated twice, once for an egg straight from the fridge and once for one at room temperature, and the gap between the two graduations is the whole cost of the fridge.

Egg
57 g
In the pan
4 min 30 s
Yolk edge, from fridge
62.8 °C
From the room
69.4 °C

Both values are the formula below, solved exactly for the line you have drawn: the same egg and the same minutes, started at 4 °C or at 21 °C.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the egg’s weight in grams, shell and all. The brackets beside the ruler are the weight classes printed on European boxes, small to very large.
  2. Set the right ring at the minutes the egg will spend in water that is already boiling.
  3. Read the temperature at the edge of the yolk where the line crosses the middle scale: on its left for an egg from the fridge, at 4 °C; on its right for an egg left out at 21 °C.
  4. To go the other way, choose a yolk above. The right ring moves to the time that gives it, for the weight you have set.

Remarks

Look at the two graduations on the middle scale before you read either. The 63 on the fridge side stands level with 69.6 on the room side: in the time it takes to bring a cold egg’s yolk edge to 63 °C, the starting point of a soft yolk, the same egg from the counter would have reached 70, where the yolk is a soft, waxy solid. Then look how the offset changes going up the scale. At the bottom the two graduations are far apart, and near 80 they are close. That is the fridge’s cost in proportion: 40 percent more time at 63 °C, 18 percent more at 80 °C. In minutes it does not change at all. For a 57 g egg, starting at 4 °C instead of 21 °C costs 78 seconds whatever yolk you want, because the starting temperature enters the formula as a term added to the time, scaled only by the egg’s size. Press the yolks above for one egg and compare the two rows: every pair of times is 1 minute 18 seconds apart.

Now look at the spacing of the yolk degrees. They spread as they climb. On the fridge side a degree at 63 costs 11 seconds of boiling; a degree at 80 costs 20. The soft egg is the hard one to time. A quarter of a minute too long moves a soft yolk by more than a degree; from liquid at 62 °C to the start of setting at 64 is 22 seconds. A hard-boiled egg forgives twice the error. The ticks crowd towards the bottom because the formula takes the logarithm of the yolk’s remaining distance from the boiling point: each degree nearer 100 is a larger fraction of what is left.

The weight ruler is the long one, and it moves the answer least. Its full height, 35 to 85 grams, is more than a doubling of the egg, and it changes the time by 1.8 times; the time goes as the two-thirds power of the weight, because heat has to cross the egg’s radius and the radius grows only as the cube root of the weight. That is why the middle scale stands three quarters of the way across, close to the time ruler. In practice one size class, ten grams, is worth about three degrees at the yolk at 63 °C, or 32 seconds. A medium egg cooked to a large egg’s time comes out about three degrees firmer: the difference between a yolk that runs and one that has begun to set.

So the order of things that matter, read from the chart, is: the yolk you want, then whether the egg came from the fridge, then its size. A single number of minutes, given without the other two, fixes all three without saying so.

The formula treats the egg as a sphere of uniform white, put into water at a full boil at sea level, and reads the edge of the yolk, not its centre. Williams’s page reports that it underestimates the extra time needed at altitude, where water boils below 100 °C, and the chart is drawn for 100 °C only. For a 57 g egg from the fridge it gives 4 minutes 32 seconds to reach 63 °C, 3 minutes 59 seconds for a 47 g egg and 5 minutes 3 seconds for a 67 g egg; the page rounds these to 4½, 4 and 5. From 21 °C it gives 3 minutes 14 seconds for the 57 g egg, where the page says about 3½.

Formula

t = 0.451 · M2/3 · ln[0.76 (Tegg − Twater) / (Tyolk − Twater)], with t in minutes and M in grams; Charles Williams derived it from the cooling of a sphere, keeping the first term of the series solution, which is where the 0.76 comes from.

The constant is cρ1/3 / (Kπ²(4π/3)2/3) for the egg’s specific heat c, density ρ and thermal conductivity K; 0.451 is the value that reproduces the worked times on Williams’s page. Write the logarithm as KT; then log KT = log t − ⅔ log M − log 0.451, a sum.

The outer rulers are logarithmic, the weight ruler runs downward to turn its minus sign into a plus, and the middle scale stands where its distances to the outer rulers are in the ratio of ⅔ to 1 once each ruler’s length per decade is allowed for.

With Twater = 100 °C, the fridge graduation is KT = ln[72.96 / (100 − Tyolk)] and the room graduation ln[60.04 / (100 − Tyolk)]. They differ by ln(96/79) = 0.195 everywhere, a constant amount of KT and so a constant number of minutes for a given egg, 0.088 M2/3.
