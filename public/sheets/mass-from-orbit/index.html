Mass from an orbit

How heavy a planet or a star is, from how far one of its moons circles it and how long the circle takes. No one has put Jupiter on a balance. Its mass is read from its moons, and on this chart all four of them say the same thing at once.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the moon’s distance from the centre of the body it circles, in thousands of kilometres (strictly, the semi-major axis of the orbit).
  2. Set the right ring at the time it takes to go round once, in days, or in years on the ruler’s inner side.
  3. Read the mass where the line crosses the middle scale: on its right in Earth masses, and on its left the bodies the measured orbits point to.
  4. Or press a name above to set both rings to that orbit.

Remarks

Press Io, then Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. The rings travel a long way: Callisto is four and a half times as far out as Io and takes more than nine times as long to go round. The crossing on the middle scale does not move. Four orbits, four different lines, one point, and the point is Jupiter: 318.1, 318.0, 317.9 and 317.9 Earth masses from the four of them. Now press Mimas and the five other moons of Saturn, and the four planets of the Sun. Each family of thin lines is a pencil, lines through one point, and there are six such points on the middle scale.

That picture is Kepler’s third law. Kepler published in 1619 that the cube of a planet’s distance from the Sun divided by the square of its period is the same number for every planet. A calculator shows that as a column of nearly equal quotients. The chart shows it as concurrence: the lines of a whole family meet, and what they meet at is a property of the body in the middle, not of any one orbit. Newton gave the point its name, since the constant is the central mass times the gravitational constant over 4π². Moving along a pencil is moving outward around one planet; jumping from pencil to pencil is changing planets.

Look where the middle scale stands: two fifths of the way from the distance ruler to the period ruler. The two outer rulers give five decades each to the same length of paper, and the mass goes as the cube of the distance and the inverse square of the period, so the scale sits nearer the quantity that counts three times. And it is short. A decade of mass takes one fifth of the paper of a decade of distance: from a Pluto to a Sun, eight decades, fits in a third of the height. Mass is a coarse thing to read and a sensitive thing to measure. Misjudge the distance by one percent and the mass moves three; misjudge the period by one percent and it moves two.

The same exponents put lines from different families nearly through one point on the period ruler. Titan takes 15.9 days to go round Saturn and Callisto 16.7 to go round Jupiter, so their lines almost meet on the right. On the left they part: Callisto is 1.54 times as far out. The cube of 1.54 is 3.66; take out the square of the slightly longer period, 1.10, and the 3.34 left over is the ratio of Jupiter’s mass to Saturn’s that the middle scale gives.

A line reads the mass of everything inside the orbit, the two bodies together. That hardly matters for Jupiter’s moons, but press Moon: the line reads 1.009 Earth masses, not 1, because the Moon is about one eighty-first of Earth and is weighed along with it. Press Charon and the line reads Pluto and Charon together, 0.00245 Earth masses, of which Charon is about a ninth. The bodies named on the middle scale are placed at these pair masses where a moon is heavy enough to matter.

The constant is G = 6.6743 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²; one Earth mass is 5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg. Orbits are taken as circles of radius equal to the semi-major axis, which Kepler’s law allows for any ellipse. Phobos, at 9 378 km and 0.319 days, reads 0.1076 Earth masses for Mars, and Deimos 0.1075; NASA gives 0.1074.

Formula

M + m = 4π²a³/(GT²), Newton’s form of Kepler’s third law, with a the semi-major axis and T the period. In the chart’s units, M (Earth masses) = 1.3268 × 10⁻¹⁴ · a³ / T² with a in kilometres and T in days. Its logarithm is a sum, log M = 3 log a − 2 log T + log 1.3268 × 10⁻¹⁴, so the outer rulers are logarithmic, the period ruler runs downward to make the minus sign a plus, and the middle scale stands where the ratio of its distances to the outer rulers is 2 to 3.
