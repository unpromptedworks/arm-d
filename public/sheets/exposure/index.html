Exposure

The light in the scene, the film or sensor speed, the aperture and the shutter time: four quantities in one formula, and a three-scale chart can hold only three. So this one uses two straight lines. The first ends on a dashed line that carries no figures; the second turns about the point where the first ended. That point is the whole answer, and it has a name.

Directions

  1. Set the left ring at the film speed, the ISO setting. Faster films stand lower.
  2. Set the second ring at the light in the scene. The names on its left are typical scenes from the American exposure guide; the figures on its right are their average luminance in candelas per square metre, what a light meter measures.
  3. The first line stops on the dashed pivot line. Don’t read it; it is a hinge.
  4. Set the aperture ring, on the right-hand ruler (smaller apertures stand lower), and read the shutter time where the line from the hinge to it crosses the ruler between them (faster times stand higher). Or set the shutter and read the aperture: whichever of the two you set last is held, and the other follows when you change the left side.

Remarks

Look at the middle of the chart. Every other sheet on this site has three scales, because a straight line through two known points can find only one unknown. Exposure has four quantities, so the chart is two three-scale charts set back to back, sharing the dashed line in the middle. The first line takes the film speed and the light and fixes a point on it. The second starts at that point and takes the aperture to find the shutter. Such a line is called a pivot line or a tie line, and it is usually left without figures, since nothing on it needs reading; Ron Doerfler, describing four-variable charts, calls the point on it the pivot point.

Now tick the box. The pivot line gets numbers, and they are exposure values, EV = log₂(N²/t). The German shutter maker Friedrich Deckel developed the scale in the 1950s; in Europe it was sold as the Light Value System. A pivot line carries whatever the two halves of a formula have in common, and here the common quantity was worth a name, because it is the only thing the scene and the camera need to agree on. The scene and the film speed fix the exposure value; the lens and the shutter can then share it out between them any way they like.

Move the aperture ring and watch them share it. The second line turns about the pivot point and the shutter ring follows: a stop smaller on the aperture, a stop longer on the shutter. Every line through that point is the same exposure, and the readout lists them at whole stops. Change the film speed or the light instead and the pivot point slides along the line; the ring you set last on the right stays where it is and the other takes up the whole change, as a camera does in aperture priority or shutter priority.

The sunny sixteen rule is one of those lines. It says: on a sunny day, set f/16 and a shutter speed of one over the film speed. Put the light at full sun, the aperture at f/16, and slide the film speed. The shutter ring moves third for third with it, because with the aperture held, each third of a stop the film gains, the shutter has to give back. So the rule works at any film speed, and the chart says why. It also says the rule is a third of a stop out from the American guide: where the rule gives 1/100 at ISO 100, 1/400 at ISO 400, the chart’s full sun gives 1/125 and 1/500, at every speed. One of the two is rounded, and the chart cannot say which.

The rulers are drawn at exact stops and labelled the way cameras label them. 1/125 sits where 1/128 belongs, f/11 where f/11.3 would, ISO 125 where 126 would. A camera’s numbers are names for positions on a scale of doublings, and the chart reads only positions.

Last, set the light at full moonlight, about EV −2, with ISO 100 and f/16. The shutter would need more than a quarter of an hour, and the ruler ends at 30 seconds. The ring stops at the end of its ruler, the line runs on past it, and the readout says how many stops dark the picture would be. Open the aperture or raise the film speed and the line comes back on.

Formula

The exposure equation for a reflected-light meter is N²/t = LS/K, with N the f-number, t the exposure time in seconds, L the average scene luminance in cd/m², S the ISO arithmetic speed and K the meter’s calibration constant, here 12.5. Taking base-2 logarithms, EV = log₂(N²/t) = log₂(LS/K); at ISO 100 the luminance is L = 2EV − 3 cd/m², so full sun at EV 15 is about 4,100 cd/m². In stops, EV = 2 log₂ N − log₂ t, which the APEX system of 1960 writes Ev = Av + Tv: everything adds, and every ruler is uniform in stops.

Construction. Each half of the chart is an ordinary three-scale sum chart, like Sheet 00’s, with the pivot line as one of its outer scales. The pivot line is drawn at 30.6 units per stop, from EV −2 to 22. The light stands 0.6 of the way from the film speed to the pivot line, and the shutter 0.6 of the way from the aperture to it. For a straight line to add, the far outer scale must then run the opposite way at 1.5 times the pivot line’s stop, which is why higher film speeds and smaller apertures stand lower, and the middle scale the same way at 0.6 times it. The middle scales are always the compressed ones, so the two widest ranges, the light’s eighteen stops and the shutter’s seventeen, are put in the middle.
