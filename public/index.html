Index Line Working nomograms

Lay a line across two scales. Read the third.

A nomogram is a chart that computes. Each scale stands for one quantity in a formula, and any straight line that crosses them all lands on values that satisfy it. For about seventy-five years they did quick, accurate arithmetic in engineering offices, pharmacies and aircraft cockpits. Then pocket calculators arrived.

This site draws them again, one formula to a sheet, and works them in the browser: drag a ring and the red line, the index line, moves with it. The claim of the site is that the old instrument is still the better way to understand a formula, if not to compute one. A calculator gives the answer and hides the shape. A nomogram prints the shape. Where its ticks crowd together the formula is slack; where they spread apart it is sensitive; where two scales stand tells you how the inputs trade off. Each sheet comes with remarks on what its scales show.

The name

The index line, also called the isopleth, is the line a straightedge makes when you lay it across a nomogram. Here it is a red stroke. The French engineer Maurice d’Ocagne is credited with inventing nomography in 1884. Everything else on one of his sheets is printed once and never changes; the index line is the only part that moves.
