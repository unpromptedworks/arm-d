Forms of chart

Every alignment chart reads one fact: three marks lie on one straight line. The forms differ only in where they put the marks. Here is one lens drawn three ways and one quadratic drawn two ways, all working, so the forms can be compared on the same numbers.

Remarks

Look at the ticks before the lines. Drawing A crowds its ticks: the object scale puts everything from 20 cm to 60 cm in a sixth of its length, and its ruler is the reciprocal 100/u, so the equation 1/u + 1/v = 1/f becomes a sum and the middle scale is an average. Drawing B spaces every centimetre alike, on all three scales. It works because of similar triangles: the line from u on one leg to v on the other crosses the diagonal at the point whose two coordinates are both f, and f/u + f/v = 1 is the thin-lens equation multiplied by f. B is the easier chart to read and the worse one to learn from. It hides the reciprocal in its geometry, and it runs out of paper at 60 cm, where A has already reached infinity.

Now hold the lens in mind and move only the object. In A and B the line turns about one fixed point on the middle scale: a lens is a point. In C the lens is a curve, and focusing it is walking along that curve. That is the exchange d’Ocagne made. On a Cartesian chart a reading is a point where two coordinates meet and a relation is a curve; on his chart a reading is a line and a relation of this kind is a point the lines pass through. Each curve in C meets the square’s diagonal at u = v = 2f, where the image is the size of the object.

C shows something A and B cannot: every answer at once. One glance says the 10 cm lenses lie on one bend and the 25 cm lenses far out in the corner, and a reader can see each curve flatten as it runs out along an axis, where a large change in the object distance moves the image distance hardly at all. What C cannot do is read a value between its curves. With the point between the 10 and the 15 the reader must guess the fraction, and the readout above only says which two curves it lies between. A and B give a number at every setting. A fourth quantity would cost C a family of curves for each of its values; it costs a nomogram one more scale and a pivot line, as on Sheet 08.

One quadratic, two drawings

Directions

  1. In D, set the left ring at u and the right ring at v. Read w where the line crosses the curve.
  2. In E, the same two numbers set the line; it crosses the circle at both roots.
  3. Keep u at −4 and raise v to 4, then past it. Watch E.

Remarks

Drawing D keeps u and v on straight parallel rulers, and then the marks for w will not stand in a line. They lie at x = 1/(1 + w), y = −w²/(1 + w) across the unit square between the rulers, which is a curve; check by putting those points into the line from u to v, and the condition comes out as the quadratic. The curve says where the quadratic is sensitive: near w = 0 one unit of w takes about four and a half times as much curve as from 6 to 7, so large roots are read coarsely. And the curve holds only roots from 0 upward. At the default setting the line crosses it twice, at 3 and at 1, both roots; set u to 2 and v to −8 and the roots are 2 and −4, and D shows only the 2. A negative root has to be found by subtraction, as −u − w.

Drawing E bends the root scale into a whole circle, which holds every w from minus to plus infinity, with 0 at the foot and ±∞ at the top. The line now crosses the circle at both roots, whatever their signs. Raise v toward 4 with u at −4 and the two crossings slide together; at 4 the line just touches the circle at 2, the repeated root, and past 4 it misses, because the roots are no longer real. The sign of the discriminant, u² − 4v, is drawn here: whether the line cuts the circle, touches it or passes it by. Ron Doerfler gives this behaviour for a circular nomogram of the same equation; the drawing here is derived afresh, so its scales are placed differently from his.

How any of them is made

Write each variable’s mark as a point: (x₁(u), y₁(u)), (x₂(v), y₂(v)), (x₃(w), y₃(w)). Three points lie on one line when the determinant of the rows [x y 1] is zero. A chart exists for an equation when the equation can be rewritten as that determinant, with each row holding one variable only. Nomographers then change the determinant by row operations and by projective transformations, which keep straight lines straight, until the scales fall where they read well. D and E are an example: E is D after one such transformation, which sends D’s two parallel rulers to a tangent and a diameter and its curve to a circle, and a straight line still works on it. The forms in the table are the shapes the determinant takes most often.

Still in use

Doerfler, writing in 2009, found nomograms “scattered at best”: simple charts doctors use for probabilities and quantities such as body-mass index, some in science and engineering papers, and some in manufacturers’ catalogues. He notes that the nomogram outlived the slide rule, its contemporary. The slide rule did general arithmetic in a sequence of steps; a nomogram does one equation in one step.
